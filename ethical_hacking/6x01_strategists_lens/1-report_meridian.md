## 1. Executive Summary
Meridian Federal Bank is in the middle of a materially risky transition from a legacy mainframe environment to a hybrid AWS and on-premises architecture. The primary concern is not a single software flaw but the temporary state created by dual-running systems during the migration window, when identity boundaries, secrets, and data-validation controls are most likely to be inconsistent across the estate. Using STRIDE, this engagement focused on how spoofing, tampering, data disclosure, denial-of-service conditions, and privilege escalation could occur across the migration boundary between legacy and modern systems. The highest-priority risks are shared credentials and weak integrity controls across integration flows, both of which could permit unauthorized data movement, transaction alteration, or operational disruption without a clear trail of accountability. These are engineering issues rather than board-level governance issues, and they are actionable within the current architecture program if addressed before legacy decommissioning.

## 2. Engagement Context
This assessment was conducted in the context of Meridian’s mid-cycle migration from a legacy mainframe to a hybrid AWS/on-premises design, with a six-week dual-running period scheduled before legacy decommission. The objective was to identify the risks introduced by that transition state rather than to evaluate the steady-state architecture after completion. The engagement was intentionally framed to support the bank’s architecture and engineering teams in improving design decisions under SOX, GLBA, and OCC obligations, while remaining within the explicit scope of the provided RoE and the supplied architecture materials.

## 3. Framework Choice and Rationale
STRIDE was selected as the primary framework for Meridian because it aligns directly with the architecture risks created by a hybrid migration boundary. The migration architecture diagram shows a clear overlap between legacy batch and messaging flows and AWS-hosted services, which is exactly where spoofing, tampering, and privilege-escalation concerns become most visible. The business context document reinforces that Meridian is a regulated financial institution under SOX, GLBA, and OCC expectations, which makes confidentiality, integrity, and availability concerns central to the design review. The threat intelligence briefing on US financial-sector adversary activity further clarifies that identity abuse, API abuse, and service disruption are not hypothetical risks during transitions of this type. This is a more engineering-oriented choice than a pure ATT&CK or campaign-oriented approach, and it is more actionable to the architecture team than a solely business-process model. Compared with the more campaign-oriented framing used on Sundara and the technique-heavy patterning often used on Helix, STRIDE is better suited to controlling a transition-state architecture where trust boundaries are the primary risk driver.

## 4. Threat Model
The threat model considered the migration architecture as a set of trust boundaries rather than as individual system findings. The most important design risk is that the dual-running environment creates parallel identities, shared access paths, and partial validation logic between legacy batch systems and AWS integration services. These conditions increase the chance that one compromised service principal or tampered API payload could affect both the legacy and modern data paths at the same time.

| Architecture element | Primary STRIDE concerns | Risk statement | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Legacy mainframe + batch interfaces | Spoofing, tampering, repudiation | Shared or reused service credentials and weak integrity checks can allow impersonation or illicit modification of financial data while legacy jobs are still active. | High |
| AWS integration layer | Spoofing, tampering, information disclosure, privilege escalation | Overly broad IAM or trust relationships create an easy pivot path from a low-privilege workload to production-facing services and data. | High |
| On-prem branch connectivity | Denial of service, information disclosure | Network congestion or weak segmentation can degrade branch operations and expose traffic that should remain internal. | Medium |
| Data stores and staging areas | Information disclosure, tampering | Staging stores and exports can leak regulated data or allow undetected modification of records before reconciliation. | High |
| Administration and secrets management | Elevation of privilege, repudiation | Break-glass access, shared admin accounts, and weak secret handling reduce evidence quality and make misuse difficult to attribute. | High |

The most relevant threat scenarios were therefore: credential replay across the dual-running boundary, event-stream or payload tampering between legacy and AWS integration components, and compromised admin or deployment paths enabling lateral movement into core financial data. These scenarios directly map to the bank’s material concerns: transaction integrity, customer confidentiality, and operational continuity during the migration window. The model intentionally excludes Luxembourg-specific systems because the RoE and the stated engagement scope exclude that subsidiary from the assessment; they are discussed only under limitations and uncertainty.

## 5. Recommendations and Prioritization
1. Enforce least privilege and secret isolation across the migration boundary. Meridian should replace shared or reused service credentials with unique, short-lived identities for each legacy-to-AWS integration path and rotate secrets before the dual-running period reaches peak volume. This is the highest-priority engineering action because it closes the primary spoofing and privilege-escalation path without waiting for the entire migration to complete.
2. Add integrity validation to all batch files, message payloads, and API transactions between legacy and AWS services. Merely relying on network trust is insufficient in a hybrid migration. Meridian should require signed payloads, checksums, replay protection, and reconciliation controls at all handoff points so a compromised or malformed message is rejected before it reaches processing.
3. Segment admin access and centralize detection for the migration environment. The architecture team should isolate break-glass procedures, require MFA and approval workflows for privileged use, and send all migration-related logs to a centralized SIEM with retention tuned for SOX/GLBA evidence requirements. This reduces the risk that one administrative compromise becomes a bank-wide incident.

The priority order is driven by both the STRIDE assessment and the operational context of a financial institution in a transition state: identity control comes first, integrity validation second, and observability plus segmentation third. These recommendations are practical, attainable during the migration, and directly relevant to the architecture team’s immediate workload.

## 6. Limitations and Uncertainty
This engagement was limited to the materials and scope explicitly included in the project brief and RoE. The Luxembourg subsidiary was excluded by scope and is therefore not assessed in the threat model or in the findings. That exclusion is a real jurisdictional and operational limitation, and it should be treated as a boundary condition rather than as an indication of lower risk for the bank overall. The assessment also relies on the supplied architecture diagram and the engagement narrative rather than a live environment review, so operational implementation details such as CI/CD pipelines, branch network segmentation, or legacy job scheduling could alter the final risk ranking if they differ materially from the current design assumptions. The assessment is therefore strongest as a design-time review of the migration state, not as a statement of the final-state security posture after decommissioning the legacy environment.
